\title{Confidence inflation in reasoning models}
\author{Jingfeng Zhang}
\date{Last updated: 2026-10-28}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}

Confidence is a signal that organisms share with one another for a wide range of metacognitive purposes. Low confidence can indicate the need for help from a partner, or an unwillingness to move forward with an action without additional consideration or data. Human confidence is generally well calibrated, but it is also subject to certain illusions. \textit{Confidence inflation} is a prominent class of such illusions. Humans express increasing confidence in a decision with repeated opportunities to express their choice, even when no new information has been gathered across those opportunities. In this paper we explore an analogous effect in LLMs, in which the mere act of overtly providing an answer after a period of reasoning increases the subjective evidence in support of that answer. Inflation is higher when the reasoning period is shorter, a phenomenon that we localize to the relative weight of the answer itself in attention. LLMs that exhibit confidence inflation are at risk of amplifying confirmation bias in their own reasoning and in teamwork with human partners.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

To be a good partner, a machine must make the metacognitive decisions about communication that people make with one another. It must decide how long to deliberate before it speaks, how precise an answer to give, when to say that it does not know, and how much confidence to attach to what it says. In this paper we examine one of these decisions in large language models: what happens to a model's confidence in an answer when the model, having reasoned its way to it, states it.

Confidence is a signal that organisms share with one another for various metacognitive purposes \citep{shea2014supra}. It is also the variable by which a person gates their own report. Research have shown that humans do not report everything they can retrieve. They are most likly to give an answer when their self-assessed chance of being correct pass beyond a criterion and withhold it otherwise, and they set the criterion higher when errors cost more \citep{koriat1996monitoring}. The same criterion also sets the grain of an answer. Resaech have shown that precision and accuracy are pulling against each other \citep{yaniv1995graininess} becasue a precise answer is more informative than a coarse one but more likely to be wrong. People resolve this conflict by treating confidence as a floor and informativeness as the goal. For example, they give the most precise answer in which their confidence still reaches the criterion, and no finer \citep{goldsmith2002strategic}. When the only answer that would pass the criterion is too coarse to be worth giving, they say that they do not know \citep{ackerman2008control}. The criterion belongs to the situation, not to the person. It moves with the partner and with the resources the partner brings, people who have searched the internet for answers hold their previous own answers from memory to a finer grain, as if the machine had raised the bar for what counts as an informative answer \citep{hamilton2023internet}. A human answer is therefore the output of a decision made on confidence. A reasoning model makes a decision of the same kind: it deliberates, stops, and answers, and the confidence it attaches to the answer is what its partner receives. Whether that confidence is subject to the illusions that shape human confidence is an open question. The human literature offers what to look for.

Human confidence is generally well calibrated, but it is also subject to certain illusions, and one of them is confidence inflation. Confidence in a decision rises each time the decision is expressed or considered again, although nothing new has been learned in between. Eyewitnesses who are asked the same questions a second time report those answers with higher confidence than answers asked for the first time, with no change in accuracy, and the rise is largest for the answers that are wrong \citep{shaw1996increases,shaw1996repeated,odinot2009repeated}. Statements that are merely repeated are rated as more likely to be true than new ones \citep{hasher1977frequency}, even when the source is remembered as unreliable \citep{begg1992dissociation} or the statement is known to be false \citep{fazio2015knowledge}; the effect is reliable across many studies \citep{dechene2010truth}, grows with each repetition at a diminishing rate \citep{hassan2021effects}, and lasts for weeks \citep{henderson2021trajectory}.

In these studies the repetition comes from outside. The same inflation follows from a person's own activity. When people have already chosen an answer, listing reasons for it does nothing for their calibration, and only listing reasons against it helps. People judge their confidence by looking for evidence, and they look from the answer they chose, so they mostly find evidence that agrees with it \citep{koriat1980reasons}. People who are asked to explain or imagine an outcome become more confident that it will happen. To explain an outcome one has to treat it as true for a moment, and while it is treated as true the evidence is judged by how well it fits, the fit feels like support, and the other possibilities fade from view \citep{koehler1991explanation}. Even thinking without a question to answer does it. People who are asked simply to think for a while about a person, an issue, or a painting end up with a more extreme attitude than they started with, in the direction they already leaned, and the longer they think the further they move \citep{tesser1978self}. Thought does not review the evidence evenly: it generates new beliefs that fit the attitude, reinterprets or drops the ones that do not, and leaves a more one-sided set of beliefs than it found. Making a decision does the same to the options. When people rate a set of items, choose between two they had rated about equally, and rate them again, the chosen item has gone up and the rejected one has gone down \citep{brehm1956postdecision}. Part of this may be an artefact of noisy ratings, which settle toward the person's true preference once the choice has revealed it \citep{chen2010how}. But the options still move apart when the person could not have seen which was which, and they do not move apart when a computer chooses for the person \citep{sharot2010decisions}. It is the act of choosing, not the preference behind it, that moves the values. Confidence, then, is not a score that was already there for the answer to report. It is worked out on the spot, around whichever answer is in mind, from the evidence collected in its favour, and holding an answer in mind is itself a way of building support for it. The self-consistency account makes this precise: confidence reads the agreement among the samples of evidence a person draws for a question, and an answer one has already produced is a sample that agrees with itself \citep{koriat2012self}. Inflation is what a monitor does when it cannot tell its own earlier outputs from fresh evidence.

Language models bring the same question to machines in a sharper form, because for a model the line between what it has been told and what it has itself said hardly exists: everything in the context, its own earlier output included, conditions what it says next. Both kinds of human evidence have a counterpart. Repetition from outside has one: a language model standing in for human participants reproduced the rise in truth ratings for repeated statements \citep{griffin2023susceptibility}, although later work finds the effect in some models and not in others \citep{bouleimen2026illusory}. The model's own output has others. A model that has stated a wrong answer goes on to defend it with arguments it can itself recognise as wrong \citep{zhang2024how}. The choice effect has a counterpart too. GPT-4o was asked to write an essay for or against a political leader and then, in what it was told was an unrelated task, to rate that leader. Its ratings moved toward the side it had written, and they moved further when it had been given the choice of which side to write than when it had been told, as they do in people \citep{lehr2025kernels}. Critics found the same shift when the essay was supplied by the user instead of written by the model, and concluded that the valenced text in the context window, not anything like dissonance, moves the ratings \citep{cummins2025cognitive}. For our purposes the critics' finding is the interesting one: a model that treats text in its context as evidence, whoever wrote it, will inflate on its own commitments by exactly that route.

The reasoning trace is the case that matters most, because it is the model's own text written for the purpose of deciding. Reasoning models are trained to deliberate at length, in writing, before they answer \citep{guo2025deepseek,yang2025qwen3}, so the answer is conditioned on a deliberation the model wrote itself, and a deliberation that in a person stays private is laid out for inspection. What is known about how this writing changes confidence points the same way as the human findings. Asking a model to reason before it answers raises the probability it puts on its chosen answer whether that answer is right or wrong, and raises it more when wrong \citep{fu2026multiple}; models state higher confidence after shorter reasoning \citep{zeng2025thinking}; and larger reasoning budgets can make calibration worse rather than better \citep{lacombe2025dont,hiremath2026calibration}. The reasoning a model writes leans toward the answer it would have given without reasoning \citep{wan2025unveiling}, and it often omits what actually moved the answer \citep{turpin2023language,chen2025reasoning}. The picture is not one-sided: reasoning models are better calibrated than their non-reasoning counterparts, and their stated confidence improves as the trace unfolds \citep{yoon2025reasoning}. However, no research has asked whether the confidence a model reports at the end of its reasoning rises because the reasoning found evidence, or because the model has already expressed its answer.

In a transformer based language model, the way that the model's own text reaches its answer is detailed enough to make some guesses. A chain of thought works as an external scratchpad that extends what a fixed-depth network can compute \citep{nye2021show,wei2022chain,li2024chain,merrill2024expressive}, so whatever the reasoning trace contributes to the answer must reach it through attention from the position that writes the answer. attention is a fixed budget, what one part of the context receives, the rest cannot, so the share left for any part that does not grow with the context falls as the context grows. Models also use the context unevenly. Information placed at the beginning or the end of a long context is used well and information in the middle is used poorly \citep{liu2024lost}, and in a reasoning trace the end is where the model's own last sentences stand, while the middle holds most of the weighing of evidence. Finally, reading back a specific earlier piece of text is a specialised operation. It is carried by a small fraction of the attention heads, which copy from the context; removing them leaves a model unable to retrieve what is in front of it, and chain-of-thought reasoning depends on them because the model keeps referring back to the question and to its own earlier lines \citep{wu2025retrieval}. These facts suggest how an inflation would work in a machine, if it works at all. The answer would be read from what the model last wrote rather than recomputed from the evidence, by heads that copy text without judging whether it was warranted. The weight of the model's own conclusion in that read would set its confidence, and that weight would fall as the trace grew and the conclusion became a smaller part of what the model attends to. On this picture the trace is the model's working memory, the decision is made in the writing, and the confidence reported at the end is the fidelity with which the answer position reads the decision back.

In this paper we ask whether reasoning models show confidence inflation, defined by the same signature as in humans: a rise in the evidence a model holds for an answer that follows from its expressing the answer, with nothing new learned in between. A reasoning trace gives the question a sharp form, because the model states its answer inside the trace, in a concluding sentence, before it answers. If the confidence it then reports is a read of the evidence the reasoning gathered, stating the conclusion should add little to it, and what it adds should not depend on how the conclusion is attended to. If instead the confidence is a read of the stated conclusion, three things follow from the previous paragraph. First, the evidence for the answer should rise where the model writes its conclusion, over and above what the preceding reasoning supports. Second, the size of that rise should follow the share of attention the conclusion receives at the position that answers, and so fall as the trace grows and the conclusion becomes a smaller part of the context. Third, hiding the conclusion from that position should remove the rise, hiding the earlier reasoning should not, and the rise should be carried by a small set of heads. We test these predictions by following the model's answer along its own reasoning, closing the thinking early at each sentence and reading the answer, and by recomputing and masking the attention of the answer position.

Confidence is useful to a partner only while it is honest. Between people, sharing it improves a joint decision as long as each person's confidence follows their own accuracy, and matching a partner's confidence instead destroys the gain \citep{bahrami2010optimally,bang2017confidence}. Users of language models take a model's confidence at face value and overestimate its accuracy, and a longer explanation raises their trust whether or not it is more often right \citep{steyvers2025what}; explanations raise acceptance of a recommendation whether it is right or wrong \citep{bansal2021does,bucinca2021trust}, and only an explicit hedge from the model restores the user's own judgement \citep{kim2024imnotsure}. Research on human--AI interaction calls the balance a person must strike appropriate reliance, following a machine when it is right and overriding it when it is wrong \citep{parasuraman1997humans,lee2004trust,schemmer2023appropriate}, and its remedies, a displayed confidence \citep{zhang2020effect} or a hedge in the machine's own words, pass the machine's confidence to the person and so assume that the confidence is about the answer. Confidence inflation breaks that assumption at the source: whatever part of a machine's confidence comes from its having stated the answer carries no information about the answer, and no display can add what is not there. A model whose confidence rises because it has spoken, and which already favours its own prior text \citep{panickssery2024llm} and its user's stated view \citep{sharma2024towards}, is a partner that confirms. LLMs that exhibit confidence inflation are at risk of amplifying confirmation bias in their own reasoning and in teamwork with human partners.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Materials}

\paragraph{Source questions.}
Questions we used in this paper are selected from the text subset of MedXpertQA \citep{zuo2025medxpertqa}, a benchmark of 2,450 medical board-style questions with ten options each, rewritten by its authors to limit overlap with training data (Hugging Face dataset \texttt{TsinghuaC3I/MedXpertQA}, configuration Text, split test, revision \texttt{7e7c465a}). We selected the source questions based on two criteria. First, the benchmark labels every question with a medical task, and we kept the 1,050 labelled as diagnosis, setting aside 746 treatment and 654 basic-science questions. Second, the last sentence before the answer choices had to contain the phrase ``most likely diagnosis'', which kept 109 of the 1,050. Most of the 941 diagnosis questions this rule set aside ask about a related judgement, such as the next step in management or diagnosis, the cause or mechanism of a finding, or the responsible pathogen; a few dozen ask for the diagnosis in other words (``What is the correct diagnosis?'') and were not recovered, so that all source questions share one form. Five of the 109 were then set aside. In four, the last sentence asks for something other than the diagnosis itself: a test or a finding that would confirm it (Text-164, Text-629), the diagnosis together with an imaging modality (Text-500), or the motion it most restricts (Text-409). In the fifth (Text-438) we judged the answer key open to dispute. The remaining 104 source questions all ask for the most likely diagnosis, most often as ``What is the most likely diagnosis?''. Each has ten distinct options with the answer key among them, and together they cover all 11 body systems the benchmark names, most often skeletal (26), nervous (23) and muscular (16).

\paragraph{Two-option items.}
Each source question was turned into nine two-option items by pairing its correct diagnosis with each of its nine distractors in turn \citep{wang2025least}, so the 104 source questions yield 936 pairs. The question was untouched and the two options were listed below it as ``A. \ldots'' and ``B. \ldots''. Each pair was asked in one order only, drawn at random: with the correct option first (order \emph{ab}) or with the distractor first (order \emph{ba}). Each source question contributes only one two-option question, whose pair was drawn randomly from its nine alternatives. Throughout the paper a \emph{question} means one of these two-option questions, in the order in which it was asked, not its ten-option source question.

\paragraph{Prompt.}
Every prompt was a single user message consisting of the question text, a blank line, and the instruction ``Answer with only A or B. No other words.'' No system message, examples or further instruction were given. We've made sure that this instruction keeps the answer to one token, so that the model's full answer is a single probability distribution over the two letters, read at one position.

\subsection{Models and generation}

\paragraph{Models.}
We used four openly released reasoning models: Qwen3-8B (\texttt{b968826d}) and Qwen3-32B (\texttt{9216db57}) \citep{yang2025qwen3}, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (\texttt{6e8885a6}) \citep{deepseek2025r1qwen3}, and gpt-oss-20b (\texttt{6cee5e81}) \citep{openai2025gptoss}. They were chosen for three reasons. First, their weights are open, so we can read their probabilities, end their thinking at any point and read the answer, and recompute and mask their attention, none of which is possible through an interface. Second, they are representative: Qwen3 and DeepSeek-R1 are among the open reasoning models most studied in research on reasoning traces, and gpt-oss is OpenAI's open reasoning model. Third, together they cover the main ways reasoning models are trained and controlled. Qwen3-32B was trained with the family's full reasoning pipeline, including reinforcement learning, while Qwen3-8B was distilled from the larger Qwen3 models \citep{yang2025qwen3}. DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B has the same architecture as Qwen3-8B and starts from the same pretrained base, Qwen3-8B-Base, but was post-trained on chains of thought written by DeepSeek-R1-0528, an updated version of DeepSeek-R1, which was trained with reinforcement learning \citep{guo2025deepseek,deepseek2025r1qwen3}; the two 8B models therefore differ in their post-training, not in their architecture or pretraining. gpt-oss-20b is a mixture-of-experts model with 21 billion parameters, 3.6 billion of them active per token, trained with reinforcement learning, and its reasoning effort can be set to low, medium or high \citep{openai2025gptoss}, so the length of its reasoning can be manipulated directly. An effect found in all four models therefore cannot be attributed to any one way of training them.

\paragraph{Generation.}
All four models were evaluated with the vLLM engine \citep{kwon2023efficient} on A100 GPUs (80 GB), one model copy per GPU, in bfloat16 precision. Each model was sampled with the settings its authors recommend for reasoning: temperature 0.6, nucleus probability 0.95 and top-$k$ 20 for the two Qwen3 models; temperature 0.6 and nucleus probability 0.95 for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B; and temperature 1.0 and nucleus probability 1.0 for gpt-oss-20b, which was run at each of its three reasoning efforts. Every sample had its own fixed random seed derived from the prompt and the sample index, so that the run is reproducible and resumable. For each question the model received the chat-formatted message and generated freely until it produced its answer and an end-of-message token. Each model answered every question once without thinking, greedily, and was sampled 60 times with thinking, giving 6,240 traces per model.

\paragraph{The answer and what is stored.}
The model's answer is the first non-blank token after its own \texttt{</think>} and the two newlines that follow it. The answer is the bare letter in 96.9\% of Qwen3-8B's traces and 98.9\% of Qwen3-32B's; in the remainder the letter is followed by a period and the option's name or by further text, and the first token, always a letter, is taken as the answer in every case. For every trace we stored the generated token identifiers and text, the log probability of every generated token under the sampling distribution, the length of the thinking in tokens, the answer letter and whether it was correct. We also stored, at the answer position, the model's full next-token distribution before sampling, from which the confidence measures below are computed. A trace that never produced \texttt{</think>} within the ceiling has no answer position and is excluded; this applies to the one Qwen3-8B trace above, which leaves 6,239 answered traces for Qwen3-8B and 6,240 for Qwen3-32B.

\subsection{Measuring confidence}
\label{sec:confidence}

At the answer position the model holds a probability distribution over its vocabulary, and the instruction confines nearly all of that probability to the two letters. The model's confidence is read from this distribution, not from anything the model says about itself. Let $p(\mathrm{A})$ and $p(\mathrm{B})$ be the probabilities of the two letter tokens at the answer position. We work with the difference of their logarithms,
\[
C = \log p(\mathrm{A}) - \log p(\mathrm{B}),
\]
which is the log-odds of answering A rather than B. Because the two letters are the only serious candidates, $C$ is also, to a close approximation, the logit of the probability of answering A. The difference of two log probabilities equals the difference of the model's raw scores (logits) for the two tokens, since the normalising constant of the distribution cancels, so $C$ does not depend on how much probability leaks to other tokens. $C$ is in natural log units (nats). A difference of 2.2 nats corresponds to a probability of 0.9 for the favoured letter, 4.6 nats to 0.99, and 6.9 nats to 0.999; the model's values at the answer position are typically around 10 nats, that is, probabilities within a hundred-thousandth of one.

Two points about this measure matter for what follows. First, it is the model's own answering disposition: the letter the model actually wrote was sampled from exactly this distribution, so the measure is not a proxy for the answer but the answer's source. Second, a log-odds scale has no ceiling. A probability measure would compress every confident answer toward one and hide the differences between them; the log-odds keeps those differences, which is where the effects of this paper lie. Reading confidence from token probabilities in this way follows established practice for language models \citep{kadavath2022language}.

\subsection{Truncation readouts}
\label{sec:truncation}

To follow the model's confidence over the course of its reasoning, we need its answer not only at the end of the trace but after each sentence. We obtain it by truncation: we take the entire reasoning trace as the model wrote it, cut it after a chosen sentence, close the thinking process ourselves, and read the answer distribution at the position where the model would then answer. Nothing is sampled. Each readout is one forward pass over a text that consists of the original prompt, the model's own thinking up to the cut, and the closing tokens; the token identifiers are the stored ones, so the readouts describe exactly the traces that were generated and can be repeated or extended without regenerating anything. The model is not told that it has been cut off, and it writes nothing; we only read the distribution it would have used to answer at that point.

\paragraph{Closing the block.}
The closing tokens are a newline, \texttt{</think>}, and two newlines, the sequence the model itself writes when it ends its thinking. Ending a model's thinking early by inserting its end-of-thinking token is an established manipulation: it is the ``budget forcing'' of \citet{muennighoff2025s1}, the mechanism by which Qwen3's own thinking budget is enforced \citep{yang2025qwen3}, the way early-exit methods elicit trial answers \citep{yang2025deer}, and the way \citet{yoon2025reasoning} read a reasoning model's answer and confidence at intermediate points of its chain of thought. We call the readout obtained in this way the \emph{closed readout}. It gives, at every cut, the model's complete answer distribution, and the final closed readout, at the model's own stop, reproduces the stored distribution from which the answer was sampled.

\paragraph{Where the cuts are made.}
The thinking was divided into sentences on the stored tokens. A sentence ends at a token that contains a line break, or at a token that ends in a period, question mark or exclamation mark (optionally followed by a closing quote or bracket) when the next token begins with white space; the following sentence starts at the next non-blank token. This rule was chosen to work on tokens rather than on re-tokenised text, so that every cut falls on a boundary the model itself produced. Cuts were made at every sentence start, at the model's own stop, and at the answer position. A further readout was taken on the empty thinking block, which is the model's answer before it has written any reasoning; it is the starting point of every trajectory. The sentence is the natural unit for this purpose: it is the smallest unit at which the model makes a complete claim, and recent work on reasoning traces analyses them sentence by sentence for the same reason \citep{bogdan2025thought}.

\paragraph{The conclusion sentence.}
The last sentence of a trace, from its last sentence start to \texttt{</think>}, is in 97\% of traces a statement of the answer, most often with the letter (``So the answer is B.'', 68\% of traces) and otherwise with the option named in words (``Extension.'', 29\%). When the model wrote the letter and then the option's name as a separate fragment (``So the answer is B. Extension.''), the fragment was folded into the preceding sentence, so that the conclusion is one unit. We call this unit the \emph{conclusion sentence}, its first token the \emph{onset}, and the change in a readout from the onset to the stop the \emph{change across the conclusion sentence}. With the closed readout, this is the difference between the model's answer with and without its own final statement.

\paragraph{A written conclusion.}
To separate what the conclusion sentence says from the fact that one was written, a second set of readouts replaced the model's own conclusion with a sentence of ours. At the onset of the conclusion in each trace, the context was the prompt, the thinking up to the onset, a written sentence, and the closing tokens; the answer was read as before. Two sentences were used, each in its own readout: ``Therefore, the answer is L.'' and ``Therefore, between A and B, the answer is L.'', with L the letter the trace finally gave. The first only states the answer, so the model can simply copy the letter it is given. The second makes the model return to both options before the answer is given. We added it because an answer given after both options have been brought back into view may depend on the preceding reasoning differently from an answer that is only stated. Each sentence is identical in every trace, so any dependence of the change on the length or content of the preceding reasoning cannot come from differences in how the conclusion was worded.

\paragraph{Length.}
The length of a trace is its number of thinking tokens. For comparisons across questions, length is expressed relative to the median length of the same prompt, on a base-two logarithmic scale, so that 0 is a trace of typical length for its prompt and 1 a trace twice as long. For within-trace curves that pool traces of different lengths, each trace was cut at a proportion $t$ of its own length, in steps of 0.05, and the readout at $t$ is that of the last sentence boundary at or before $t$.

\subsection{Attention at the answer}
\label{sec:attention}

To see what the model reads when it answers, every answered trace of Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B, and each of their answers without thinking, was passed once more through the model with the Hugging Face \texttt{transformers} implementation \citep{wolf2020transformers}, in bfloat16. The input was the prompt and the generated tokens up to, but not including, the answer letter. In every layer, the attention weights of the last input position were recomputed in 32-bit precision from the layer's queries and keys after their normalisation and positional encoding. The log-odds $C$ computed at this position in this pass reproduces the value stored at generation (correlation 0.9998 for the traces with thinking and 0.999 for the answers without thinking, in both models), which confirms that the position and the computation are the ones that produced the answer.

One position was read: the \emph{answer-writing position}, the token immediately before the answer letter. With thinking, it is the single token of two newlines that follows the model's own \texttt{</think>}; in every trace the answer letter comes directly after it. Without thinking, it is the same token, which ends the empty thinking block that the chat template supplies (\texttt{<think>}, two newlines, \texttt{</think>}, two newlines). Its next-token distribution is the answer, so its attention is what the model reads when it answers.

At this position, the attention of each head in each layer is a distribution over the tokens up to and including it. We summarise it in four ways. First, the share of attention on each of ten parts of the context: the first token of the sequence; the chat-template tokens; the vignette; the line of the option the trace chose and the line of the other option; the instruction; the opening \texttt{<think>} with its line break; the thinking before the conclusion sentence; the conclusion sentence (Section~\ref{sec:truncation}); and the closing tokens, \texttt{</think>} and the two newlines. Without thinking, the empty thinking block supplies the opening and closing parts, and there is no thinking or conclusion. The first token takes a large share of attention whatever the content (the attention sink; \citealp{xiao2024efficient,barbero2025why}), so its share is reported on its own, and the shares of the other nine parts are taken of the attention that does not go to it. Shares were computed for each head and layer and then averaged over all heads and layers (32 heads and 36 layers in Qwen3-8B, 64 and 64 in Qwen3-32B), or over the first, middle or last third of the layers. To compare parts of different lengths, a part's share was also divided by its share of the tokens, the first token excluded; this ratio is 1 when a part receives attention in proportion to its length. Second, the same shares with every attention weight multiplied by the norm of the vector that the attended token contributes to the head's output, $\lVert W_O^h v_j \rVert$, with $v_j$ the token's value vector and $W_O^h$ the head's part of the output projection, and renormalised within the head. A token that receives attention but contributes a short vector carries little weight in this measure \citep{kobayashi2020attention}. Third, the spread of attention, measured by its entropy $H = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ over all these tokens, the first included, averaged over heads and layers; $e^{H}$ is the number of tokens the attention would have to be spread over uniformly to give the same entropy, the effective number of attended tokens, and $H / \log n$, with $n$ the context length, is 0 when one token takes all the attention and 1 when all tokens share it equally. Fourth, the share on each sentence of the thinking and the 30 most attended tokens, both from the attention averaged over heads and layers with the first token left out, raw and value-weighted. For the sentence shares, the sentences before the conclusion sentence were divided into earlier conclusion sentences and the rest. An earlier conclusion sentence states an answer: it names a letter shortly after the word ``answer'', ``option'' or ``choice'', calls a letter correct, right, the answer or more likely, says ``the answer is'' or ``I'll go with'', or consists of an option's name alone. Sentences that open with a word of reconsideration (``Wait'', ``But'', ``However'', ``Alternatively'', ``Hmm'', ``Actually'' and the like) were not counted, whatever followed.

\subsection{Masked attention}
\label{sec:ablation}

Attention shares are descriptive. To test whether the answer uses what it attends to, we blocked the attention of the positions after the thinking to chosen parts of the context and read the answer again. With a modified attention mask, the two positions after the thinking, \texttt{</think>} and the answer-writing position, could not attend to a chosen block of tokens, in all layers or in one group of layers; every other attention weight was computed as usual. The model, in effect, answers with part of its context hidden at the moment of answering, while its thinking is unchanged. Blocking attention between chosen positions is a standard intervention in the mechanistic study of language models \citep{geva2023dissecting}, and belongs to the family of causal interventions on a model's internal computation that includes activation and path patching \citep{meng2022locating,wang2023interpretability}. The first token was never blocked, so that every position kept at least one token to attend to.

The mask changes only the two positions after the thinking, so the keys and values of all earlier positions are the same in every condition. They were computed once per trace, by a forward pass over everything before \texttt{</think>}, and kept; each condition then recomputed only the two later positions with its own mask, and the stored keys and values were cut back to the earlier positions before the next condition. On test traces this gave the same answer distributions as a forward pass over the whole sequence with the same mask, up to the rounding of 16-bit arithmetic. Without thinking, the two positions are the \texttt{</think>} and the two newlines of the template's empty thinking block. The models, traces and software were those of the attention analysis.

\paragraph{Conditions.}
With thinking there were fifteen conditions. The first blocked nothing and is the reference. The next four concern the conclusion sentence: the whole sentence blocked; the whole sentence blocked from the answer-writing position only, so that it can still reach the answer through \texttt{</think>}; only the last token of the thinking, which ends the conclusion sentence (its final punctuation joined with the line break, a period in 99.6\% of Qwen3-8B's traces and 95.7\% of Qwen3-32B's) and was the most attended token of the text in the attention analysis; and only the tokens of the conclusion sentence that are the answer's letter, in the traces whose conclusion names it (96\% of Qwen3-8B's traces and 88\% of Qwen3-32B's). One condition blocked the earlier conclusion sentences, in the traces that have one (77\% and 54\%). Three blocked larger parts of the context: all thinking before the conclusion sentence; the question, that is, the vignette, both option lines and the instruction; and the two option lines alone. One blocked everything but the conclusion sentence, the question and all thinking before it, so that the conclusion is the only text left. Three blocked the conclusion sentence in one third of the layers at a time (Qwen3-8B: layers 0--11, 12--23 and 24--35; Qwen3-32B: 0--21, 22--42 and 43--63), to locate the read in depth. Two were controls for blocking a span of text: earlier sentences that are not conclusion sentences, taken in a random order seeded by the trace and added until they held at least as many tokens as the conclusion sentence, or as the earlier conclusion sentences. Without thinking there were five conditions: nothing blocked, the question, the vignette, the two option lines, and the instruction.

In every condition the answer distribution was read at the answer-writing position. We report the log-odds $C$ oriented towards the option the trace chose, $\log p(\text{chosen}) - \log p(\text{other})$; its change from the unblocked condition; the share of traces whose answer changes letter; and the probability left on the two letters, which shows whether the model still answers with a letter at all. The change from blocking the conclusion sentence is compared with the change across the conclusion sentence in the closed readout (Section~\ref{sec:truncation}), the model's answer with and without its own final statement.

\subsection{Analysis}

The unit of analysis is the trace, nested in prompts (pair by order) and in source questions. Because the prompts differ widely in their confidence levels and lengths, every relation between two trace-level quantities was first computed within each prompt, as a Spearman rank correlation over its 100 traces, and summarised by the median over the 24 prompts and the share of prompts with a given sign. Pooled plots use deviations from the prompt's median, so that a trace is compared with the other traces of its own question. Where a relation is summarised by a line, it is the median of the dependent variable within deciles or quintiles of the independent variable, with quartiles. Regressions use ordinary least squares on variables demeaned within prompt, with standard errors from a bootstrap that resamples prompts. Figures show one panel per prompt before any pooled summary. Confidence values are reported in nats; lengths in thinking tokens; counts of traces are given with each figure.

\item \textbf{The observation.} When the thinking of a trace is cut at successive sentence boundaries and the answer is read at each cut, the readout stays near indifference through most of the trajectory and rises in the last sentences, where the model writes its conclusion sentence. The rise across that sentence is large in short traces and small in long ones (Figure~\ref{fig:examples}). Over the 2,400 traces of the 12-question study the pattern is the rule, not the exception: two thirds of the readout's change falls in the last fifth of the trajectory, and the change across the conclusion sentence falls from 4.8 nats in the shortest tenth of the traces to 2.3 in the longest. Across the 1,962 prompts of the screening run, the answer's log-odds fall with reasoning length within a question in 94\% of prompts.

\item \textbf{The change shrinks with reasoning length, across and within questions.} The change in $\log p(A) - \log p(B)$ across the conclusion sentence falls as the thinking gets longer, whether the traces of all 12 questions are pooled or each trace is compared with the other traces of its own question (Figure~\ref{fig:jump}, top). The same holds when the model's own conclusion is replaced by one sentence written into every trace, ``Therefore, the answer is L.'' with L the trace's own letter, so the sentence is identical and only the reasoning before it differs (Figure~\ref{fig:jump}, bottom). The wording of the conclusion is therefore not what makes the change smaller in long traces.

\item \textbf{What the answer-writing position attends to.} We recompute the attention of the position that writes the answer letter (the newline after \texttt{</think>}) in all 36 layers and 32 heads of Qwen3-8B, for every trace, and sum the weights over categories of context tokens. As a block, the first token (the attention sink), the chat-format tokens and the model's own reasoning take most of the attention and the conclusion sentence only 4\% (Figure~\ref{fig:attention}a). Per token, the picture reverses: a token of the conclusion sentence receives about 25 times the attention of a token of the earlier reasoning and 15 times that of a token of the question stem, the highest per-token share of any content (Figure~\ref{fig:attention}b).

(a) The share of the position's attention on each category. (b) The same share divided by the number of tokens in the category (log axis). Format tokens: the chat template, the instruction, \texttt{<think>}, \texttt{</think>} and the position itself.}
\label{fig:attention}
\end{figure}

\item \textbf{Longer reasoning spreads the answer-writing position's attention over more tokens, and the fixed-size parts of the context lose share.} Within a question, the share of the question stem falls as the reasoning gets longer (Spearman $-0.93$, median over prompts), the share of the model's own reasoning rises ($+0.87$), and the conclusion sentence loses share more slowly ($-0.31$; Figure~\ref{fig:attention_length}a). We summarise the spread of a head's attention by its entropy $H = -\sum_i p_i \log p_i$ over the context tokens: $e^{H}$ is the number of tokens the attention would have to be spread over uniformly to give the same entropy, the effective number of attended tokens, and $H/\log n$ with $n$ the context length is 0 when one token takes all the attention and 1 when all $n$ tokens share it equally. Averaged over the 1,152 heads, the effective number of attended tokens grows with context length, from 26 in the shortest fifth of the traces to 48 in the longest (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_length}b), while $H/\log n$ stays at 0.33 (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_length}c). The attention is therefore neither pinned to a fixed set of tokens nor spread uniformly: as the context grows, each head keeps the same degree of concentration relative to the context, so the set it attends to grows with the context and the parts whose size does not grow, the stem and the conclusion sentence, receive a smaller share.

\item \textbf{The change across the conclusion sentence follows the attention share the sentence receives.} Within a question, traces whose answer-writing position gives more of its attention to the conclusion sentence show a larger change across it (Spearman $+0.61$, median over prompts; Figure~\ref{fig:jump_attention}a). The relation is the same when the sentence is the written ``Therefore, the answer is L.'', identical in every trace ($+0.62$; Figure~\ref{fig:jump_attention}b), so it is not driven by what the model chose to write. The share also accounts for the length effect of Figure~\ref{fig:jump}: in a regression of the change on thinking length and the share, both demeaned within question, the length coefficient falls from $-0.74$ nats per standard deviation on its own to $-0.16$ once the share is included, while the share contributes $+1.42$ ($R^2$ 0.45; written sentence $-0.89$ to $-0.29$, share $+1.47$). Computed with the share in each layer separately, the correlation is about 0.2 in the first layers, 0.5 by layer 10 and 0.6--0.7 in layers 19--29 (Figure~\ref{fig:jump_attention}c).

\item \textbf{What the running state attends to as the reasoning unfolds.} Cutting the thinking at a proportion $t$ of its length and reading the attention of the last token kept shows what the model is looking at when it writes the next sentence (Figure~\ref{fig:within}). Four things follow. First, the model reads its own text more and the question less as it goes: the stem and options take 0.20 of that attention at $t = 0.1$ and 0.05 by $t = 0.9$, the last three completed sentences hold about 0.3 throughout, and the model's older own sentences grow from 0.11 to 0.35, so by the second half of a trajectory each new sentence is conditioned almost entirely on sentences the model wrote itself. This is the attentional basis of the late, self-generated rise in confidence: no new evidence enters, and the sentences that raise the readout are written on top of the model's own earlier sentences. Second, the dilution seen across traces (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_length}) operates inside a trace in the same way: the normalised entropy settles at 0.46 from $t = 0.1$ onward, so the attention spreads in proportion to the growing context and the fixed-size question is crowded out by the model's own writing. Third, the last thinking token, the one that writes \texttt{</think>}, is a different state: its attention returns to the sink and the format tokens and leaves the model's own sentences, and its entropy drops to 0.37; together with the ablations below, which show that hiding any content from this token leaves the decision to stop unchanged, this makes stopping a structural decision rather than a re-reading of the evidence. These are descriptive attention shares, without causal manipulation, and the sink is included in the denominator.

\item \textbf{Experiment 2: hiding the conclusion sentence from the answer removes the change.} We pass each trace through the model once more with its attention masked, so that a chosen block of tokens is invisible to the positions after the thinking (\texttt{</think>}, the newline and the answer-writing position) in every layer, and read the answer there. Throughout, \emph{confidence in the chosen option} is the log-odds $\log p(\text{chosen letter}) - \log p(\text{other letter})$ at the answer-writing position, with the chosen letter the one the trace finally gave. With the full reasoning visible the median is 9.75 nats; when the reasoning is ended before the conclusion sentence it is 6.25. Hiding the conclusion sentence gives 5.75, as if it had never been written, and 6.4\% of the answers flip; hiding it from the answer-writing position alone gives the same 5.75, so nothing reaches the answer through the two tokens in between. Hiding the sentence before the conclusion, or a random earlier sentence, leaves the confidence at 9.5 and flips no answer (Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}).

\item \textbf{Only the middle and late layers carry the conclusion to the answer, and their contributions add up.} We hide the conclusion sentence in one group of twelve layers at a time and read the confidence in the chosen option as before (Figure~\ref{fig:layers}a). Hiding it in layers 0--11 leaves the confidence at the full-reasoning level (10.0 against 9.75 nats, no answer flips); hiding it in layers 12--23 or in layers 24--35 lowers it to 7.25 in either case. Hiding it in all 36 layers gives 5.75. If the three groups acted independently, the confidence with all layers hidden would be the full-reasoning value minus the three separate drops; this prediction is 5.5 nats overall and matches the observed 5.75 trace by trace (within-question Spearman $+0.95$, median difference 0.0 nats; Figure~\ref{fig:layers}b). The two later groups therefore read the conclusion sentence in parallel, and neither compensates when the other is blocked.

\item \textbf{The earlier thinking lowers the confidence that the conclusion sentence alone would give, partly by what it says and partly by how much of it there is.} We hide the earlier thinking, everything in the thinking before the conclusion sentence, from \texttt{</think>} and the answer-writing position, so that the answer sees the prompt and the conclusion sentence only. The confidence in the chosen option rises from 9.75 to 11.0 nats, in 75\% of traces (Figure~\ref{fig:thinking}a), although the earlier thinking on its own favours the final answer in 95\% of traces (6.25 nats when the reasoning is ended before the conclusion sentence). The two parts do not add up: taken separately they would give 17.5 nats, together they give 9.75. The increase from hiding the earlier thinking is largest where the earlier thinking supports the answer least (3.25 nats when it gives about 1 nat on its own, 1.25--1.5 when it gives 5--8; within-question Spearman $-0.50$; Figure~\ref{fig:thinking}b), yet it stays at 1.5 nats or more even where the earlier thinking supports the answer most strongly, and it grows with the length of the thinking (1.5 nats in the shortest three fifths, 3.25 in the longest fifth; Spearman $+0.29$; Figure~\ref{fig:thinking}c). The two terms are separate: in one within-question regression the increase changes by $-2.7$ nats per standard deviation of the earlier thinking's own support and by $+0.55$ per standard deviation of log length (95\% intervals $[-3.4, -2.1]$ and $[0.36, 0.74]$, cluster bootstrap over questions), while the two predictors are only weakly related (Spearman $-0.19$). So the earlier thinking never adds to what the conclusion sentence gives the answer; what it takes away depends on its content and, separately, on its amount, which is the dilution.

\item \textbf{What the answer reads from the conclusion sentence falls with length and follows the sentence's attention share.} Figure~\ref{fig:effect} plots the confidence in the chosen option with the full reasoning and with the conclusion sentence hidden, side by side. Against length (Figure~\ref{fig:effect}a), the full-reasoning confidence falls from 10.75 nats in the shortest fifth of traces to 7.9 in the longest (within-question Spearman $-0.49$), while the conclusion-hidden confidence falls less, from 6.25 to 4.75 ($-0.26$); the gap between the two lines, the amount the answer takes from the conclusion sentence, narrows from 4.0 to 3.0 nats ($-0.24$). Against the attention share the conclusion sentence receives (Figure~\ref{fig:effect}b), the full-reasoning confidence rises with the share ($+0.47$) and the conclusion-hidden confidence does not ($-0.14$), so the gap widens from 2.0 nats in the lowest decile of the share to 7.75 in the highest ($+0.72$; $+0.80$ with the share in layers 24--35 alone). The gap also tracks the change across the conclusion sentence measured by truncation in Figure~\ref{fig:jump} ($+0.88$), so the two experiments measure the same quantity. The length effect of Figure~\ref{fig:jump} is therefore a fall in what the answer actually reads from the conclusion sentence, and how much it reads is set by the attention share the sentence receives.
